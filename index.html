Welcome!

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at Iowa State University. I am an applied microeconomist whose research covers a range of topics in Industrial Organization, Environmental and Energy Economics, and Agricultural and Resource Economics. My research focuses on how industries adjust to long-run changes in demand and policy, and on the economic and environmental consequences of those adjustments. Research

Job Market Paper

When Mandates Outlive Markets: Selective Exit under Declining Demand in the U.S. Ethanol Industry

This paper studies how the U.S. corn ethanol industry would adjust to a projected decline in gasoline demand and how the ethanol blending mandate affects the pace and distribution of plant exit. I develop a dynamic model of ethanol plant production and exit in which plant scale and spatial heterogeneity in local corn supply affect production costs and exit incentives. The model predicts geographic differences in the industry shakeout. By 2050, expected production capacity declines by 99.4 percent in North Dakota, compared with 16.6 percent in Iowa, as larger plants in areas with less local corn supply tend to exit earlier. Raising the blend mandate from 10 to 12 percent delays exit by 5.7 years on average. The delay generates $16.6 billion in ethanol plant and local corn producer surplus, while fuel consumers have to bear $40.2 billion in additional compliance expenditure. The gains are distributed differently from the exit response. Inframarginal plants receive the largest gains, while plants near the margin of exit experience the largest delays in exit. The higher mandate also preserves local corn demand and shifts a share of the gains toward nearby corn producers.

Working Paper

Interstate Subsidy Competition under the Renewable Fuel Standard and the Blend Wall

Draft available upon request

How does declining gasoline demand affect interstate ethanol subsidy competition under the Renewable Fuel Standard and the ethanol blend wall? I develop a multi-market equilibrium model in which state governments strategically choose ethanol production subsidies under federal biofuel mandates and the blend wall, a limit on ethanol blending. I show that a state ethanol subsidy displaces production in competing states and benefits motor gasoline consumers across regulatory regimes, while its effects on biodiesel production and diesel fuel consumers depend on which regulatory constraints bind. Numerical simulations show that declining gasoline demand changes which regulatory constraints bind. When both federal mandates bind, ethanol subsidies increase biodiesel production. When the blend wall binds and the biodiesel mandate is slack, subsidy competition reduces biodiesel production and producer surplus, while consumer surplus gains become concentrated among motor gasoline consumers. As gasoline demand declines, Nash subsidies rise in major net ethanol-exporting states, while subsidy competition generates growing aggregate welfare losses for net ethanol-exporting states and gains for net ethanol-importing states relative to an equilibrium without state ethanol subsidies.

The Data Center Boom and Surface Water Quality

Draft available upon request

This paper investigates whether data center development generates negative externalities for local surface water quality. The rapid expansion of data centers has raised concerns about their environmental impacts, but empirical evidence on their effects on surface water quality remains limited. One potential pathway is through municipal wastewater systems, as wastewater from data center cooling operations may enter municipal treatment facilities and subsequently reach nearby waterways through treated effluent. To examine this channel, I construct a U.S. panel of river water quality monitoring stations from 1998 to 2025 by supplementing the open-source iM3 Data Center Atlas with operating-year information collected from public sources and linking it to municipal wastewater infrastructure, discharge records, hydrological networks, and water quality measurements. I assign data centers to municipal wastewater treatment plant service areas and use the NHDPlus river network to link wastewater discharge outfalls to downstream monitoring stations. I use a difference-in-differences (DD) design with monitoring station and river-basin-by-year fixed effects to estimate changes in water quality associated with upstream data center exposure, measured by the number and reported total area of operating facilities. Preliminary estimates vary across water quality indicators and measures of exposure, with some statistically significant associations but limited evidence of systematic deterioration in downstream water quality.
