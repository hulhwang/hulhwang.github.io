Research

When Mandates Outlive Markets: Selective Exit under Declining Demand in the U.S. Ethanol Industry

Job Market Paper

This paper studies how the U.S. corn ethanol industry would adjust to a projected decline in gasoline demand and how changes in the ethanol blending mandate alter the pace and distributional consequences of that adjustment. I develop a dynamic model of ethanol plant production and exit in which plant scale and spatial heterogeneity in local corn supply shape production costs and exit incentives. The model predicts a highly uneven geographic shakeout. Exit occurs earlier among larger plants, especially in areas with lower local corn density. Consequently, expected active capacity by 2050 falls by 99.4 percent in North Dakota compared with 16.6 percent in Iowa. Raising the blend mandate from 10 to 12 percent slows the shakeout and increases expected active years by 5.7 years on average. The resulting increase in producer surplus is smaller than the additional compliance expenditure passed through to fuel consumers. Among ethanol plants, surplus gains accrue disproportionately to inframarginal plants, whereas the largest exit delays occur among plants near the exit threshold. Keeping these plants active sustains local corn demand, shifting a larger share of the policy gains to local corn producers.
